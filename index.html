Philosophy Writing Studio

A good philosophy essay does more than state an opinion. It makes a claim, supports it, and takes objections seriously.

01 · Thesis

Answer the question

Write a specific, arguable answer to the prompt. Define key terms when their meaning matters.

02 · Argument

Show your reasoning

Explain your premises and how they support the conclusion. Give reasons, not just examples.

03 · Objection

Take disagreement seriously

Present a strong objection fairly, then explain whether your argument can answer it.

Build a mini argument

Use this workspace to sketch your own position. Your draft stays in this browser unless you copy or export it.

Tip: a strong essay engages with the best objection, not the easiest one.

Practice identifying the structure of an argument. Logic helps us ask whether a conclusion follows from its premises.

Validity or soundness?

Consider this argument:

Premise 2: Penguins are birds.
Conclusion: Therefore, penguins can swim.

Ideas across time

A short philosophy timeline

Philosophy is not a single straight line: traditions developed in different places and often overlapped.

c. 600 BCE – 500 CE

Ancient

Socrates, Plato, Aristotle, Confucius, the Stoics.

c. 500 – 1500

Medieval

Debates on faith, reason, existence, and knowledge across traditions.

c. 1500 – 1800

Early Modern

Descartes, Spinoza, Locke, Hume, Kant.

1800 – Today

Modern & Contemporary

Existentialism, analytic philosophy, feminism, mind and AI.

Read slowly, think carefully

The Reading Room

Approach philosophical texts by tracking the question, the central claim, the reasoning, and the strongest objection.

Start with a question

Before reading, write down what problem the author is trying to solve.

Map the argument

Mark the conclusion, supporting premises, assumptions, and examples.

Read against the text

Ask what a thoughtful critic might say, and whether the author has an answer.
